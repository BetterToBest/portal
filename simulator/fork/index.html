Ledger fork and halt power: when operators or guardians collude

The paper's answer to a 51% attack is a stack of safeguards (section 4.2.3): consensus must be spread across regions and institution types, and the Judicial Guard can halt a detected attack. This page tests two things: how big a colluding group of node operators must be to pass the consensus tests, and what a colluding group of guardians could do with the halt power drafted in docs/halt-power.md.

What the model assumes
  • Operators. Every node has equal weight. Each region has 20 nodes, split evenly across four institution types (universities, libraries, non-profits, individuals). The three tests are this page's reading of safeguards 3 and 4 in section 4.2.3: colluders hold a majority of all nodes, a majority in more than half of the regions, and a majority in at least three of the four types. The paper gives no numbers for these tests, so all of them are placeholders.
  • Guardians. The corps size (500 to 2,000+) is from the paper. The thresholds are the draft numbers in halt-power.md, which that document calls placeholders: a scoped hold needs 5 guardians from 3 regions (72 hours); a full pause needs 1% of the corps, at least 15, from 5 regions (24 hours); a 14-day cap; a review panel drawn by lottery from regions other than the signers'. The panel size is a placeholder of this page. Guardians are split evenly across regions.
  • The model gives the colluders the best case: it assumes they can supply the evidence a hold needs. In the draft, a trigger must be checkable by an independent node, so a false hold would also have to get past that test.
  • A hold never deletes or changes ballots, and the voting window is extended by the length of the hold (halt-power.md). The "stall" figures show delay only.
  • Not modeled: proof-of-stake slashing and its cost to attackers, the time delays in safeguard 5, the multiple competing implementations in section 4.2.1, the 10% per-location and 5% per-funder limits in section 4.2.2, and colluders who bribe or capture honest participants.

Illustrative model, not a prediction. It tests proposed rules against colluding groups that you size and place; it does not estimate how likely a real attack is. Every rule and number is a proposal from the paper or a placeholder in the draft docs, and CIP is not live.
